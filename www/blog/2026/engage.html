Burger.

Unfortunately, nobody gets left behind.

Written February 19, 2026

If men learn this, it will implant forgetfulness in their souls. They will cease to exercise memory because they rely on that which is written, calling things to remembrance no longer from within themselves, but by means of external marks.

What you have discovered is a recipe not for memory, but for reminder. And it is no true wisdom that you offer your disciples, but only the semblance of wisdom, for by telling them of many things without teaching them you will make them seem to know much while for the most part they know nothing. And as men filled not with wisdom but with the conceit of wisdom they will be a burden to their fellows.

This was one of Plato's argument against writing. As in, against the entire concept of written language. That writing information down wouldn't help people remember, just remind them. To him, it was a vital difference. If you don't internalize what you learn, instead relying on something external to keep track of it, then you haven't truly learned anything.

The irony is that I only know his words as writing. It's hard to argue writing has been bad for philosophy, science, or humanity as a whole. The sheer breadth of ideas we can examine, sometimes thousands of miles and years away from their sources, is incredible. And having those ideas written as things we can see and manipulate piecewise is vital for more advanced science and math. Still, I wonder if something is lost in our written world. Can you imagine memorizing the entirety of Homer's epics and reciting them, word for word, to pass it onto the next generation? I can't.

I recently watched a YouTube video that I found moving. A week or so later, I wanted to watch it again. Unfortunately, I forgot the title of the video. I also forgot the name of its creator. No matter, I probably added it to my Favorites. I turned out I hadn't. Well, surely I liked the video. I checked my list of liked videos. There were hundreds from the past week. I didn't remember watching most of them. Do I really watch so many shorts? I couldn't find the one I was looking for. Was it removed? I gave up for the time being.

Another week or so later, I was still bothered by this. If I can't find a video I enjoyed just two weeks ago, something that spoke to me, what does that say about me? I redoubled my efforts. I'd subscribed to the animator, maybe I'll recognize him in my subscriptions. When did I subscribe to so many hundreds of channels? I don't even watch most of these people. He also made a video where a student gives a presentation on chicken farming. Maybe I can find that. I also forgot that video's title. I tried searching by its content: classroom, battery cages, surreal animation. I asked Duck.AI, giving it a summary. It confidently gave me the title of a film that doesn't exist.

I didn't remember, but eventually I was reminded. I found the video about chickens in my Favorites playlist: “Existential Crisis in Class” by WhatsItLike. From that, I found the video I was looking for: “Tears in the Robot Factory”. Hopefully I'll make an effort to remember the things I care about going forward.

Tears in the Robot Factory

I would recommend watching “Tears in the Robot Factory” to set the mood for the rest of the blog post.

Most scam services suffer from the same problem: They waste most of their time trying to scam other accounts that appear to be human, but are actually just other AI-generated scam accounts. Our AI agents focus on scamming real humans. That's the Real Human Victim Guarantee!

“Tears in the Robot Factory” is a short film about a small group of factory workers discussing the rapidly-approaching future where they're rendered useless by AI, interspersed with a montage of horrific advertisements and news clips one worker's watching on her phone.

It paints a picture of the world similar to what I feel. The rise of large language models and other AI technology hasn't created many new problems, but it's exacerbated problems we've been seeing more and more in the modern era:

  • An inability to trust any information. A “post-truth” world.
  • Paradoxically, often a total inability to do research: taking Google's often-wrong summaries of search results as fact, for example.
  • Increased loneliness and cynicism, where cultures are fragmented and negativity is rewarded.
  • A flood of low-quality content supported by ad revenue.
  • Black-box automated systems controlling people's lives: automated bans on social media, customer service bots designed to waste your time, not being able to park because the parking lot switched to a dumbass app that took 20 minutes to set up an account for and it just crashes when you try to actually pay for the parking.
  • Tech companies making much of their money through manipulation and abuse: addictive design, dark patterns, and misusing private data.
  • Automation and technology centralizing power and resources into fewer and fewer hands.

Modern AI has primarily been used to increase all of these ills. The black boxes are deeper and more inscrutable, the slop more bountiful, the fakes more convincing.

In a Forbes article breathlessly describing the AI customer service startup Sierra, it mentions “they take care of the more knotty customer service issues — like returning a pair of shoes or canceling a subscription”. Now, perhaps I'm being willfully ignorant for the sake of argument, but canceling a subscription seems like a pretty straight-forward customer service issue to me: the customer asks to cancel their subscription, and then you cancel it. Anything else would be brow-beating the customer into paying for a service they no longer want, and surely nobody would consider that ethical, right?

In another Forbes article about a similar startup called PolyAI (Forbes loves articles about customer service AIs), the CEO says “our AI picks up every phone call, they always book you in and they never forget to upsell you.” Finally, a world where they never forget to upsell me. The future is here. I'm glad I wasn't left behind in the cowboy times. I'm glad I wasn't born as a cool cowboy and instead was born to tell a robot “no, I don't want to prepay for gas” eight times before it gives me the rental car I already paid for.

Now, it's one thing to cause the internet to contract into an even more lonely, isolating place. It's one thing to use advanced AIs mostly to rip off your customers more efficiently. But I draw the line at buying all the RAM.

Computers are Expensive and I'm Mad About It

What is the solution?

Beyond bombing ourselves back into hunter-gatherers so we can try this whole thing again from the beginning, I don't know.

It's not “Argumentum Ad Hominem” Unless it's an Argument. This is Just Verbal Abuse.

Nobody told me I had to get a smartphone or be “left behind”. It was a more useful tool that I wanted.

Nobody's ever called me a Luddite for my crusty-ass website, or blocking JavaScript by default, or driving a car from 2006, or playing pirated PS2 games instead of Umamusume or something, or using metal and wood cookware instead of plastic, or when I wrote a similar blog post to this booing “web3”.

Hell, if I switched to a flip-phone, I bet people would mostly be impressed I can survive in a smartphone-first world.

Yet people hurl insults at the lightest doubts about the utility or ethics of AI.

LLMs and other modern techniques are impressive technologies, and people have found ways to use them. They aren't useful for everything, though, and there are several reasons, for matters of ethics or quality, that somebody wouldn't want to use them.

I believe AI has so many mean, pushy advocates because it's a matter of ego. They don't want to be seen as stupid for relying on it. It's the Luddites who are stupid for staying behind.

Well, here's my take: if you use generative AI and like its output, my first assumption is that you're an idiot. You can try to prove me wrong, but confirmation bias is my specialty.

I imagine you writing two sentences of thought to generate paragraph after paragraph of mealymouthed nonsense, and then saying to yourself “you took the words right out of my mouth!”

I imagine you generating thousands of lines of code you don't understand, then making a PR for someone else to figure out if it works.

I imagine you spending college semesters copying and pasting text between a chatbot and your homework, leaving the university without an ounce of new knowledge.

I imagine you flooding art websites with dozens of generated images from the same prompt, incapable of fixing obvious flaws or even curating the output.

I imagine you anthropomorphizing large language models: writing prompts in the second person (“You write code with tests”, “You are helpful and professional”), asking it to explain why it made a decision and believing its generated output, criticizing people for “dehumanizing” something that is not human or sentient.

I imagine you giving an AI agent control of your wife's computer to organize her desktop, and it deletes all her photos.

I imagine you running to a chatbot at the first sign of resistance in any task, becoming incapable of learning, and spending the rest of your life paying a subscription for the ability to think.

The fact that people rely on these AIs, despite all their limitations, is an indictment on their competence and taste. You're filled not with wisdom, but with the conceit of wisdom, and you're a burden on your fellows.
